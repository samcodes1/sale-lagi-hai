# Logo principles: notes from practice

These are paraphrased from secondary coverage (interviews, articles); see SKILL.md Sources. Verify exact quotes
against primary sources before publishing them.

## Paul Rand ("Logos, Flags, and Escutcheons", AIGA, 1991)
- A logo identifies; it rarely describes. It does not need to be self-explanatory.
- It gets its meaning from the quality of the company it stands for, not the other way round. A great mark cannot
  rescue a weak product, and a simple mark grows rich through association.
- Practical consequence: stop adding meaning to the drawing. Add it through use.

## Sagi Haviv (Chermayeff & Geismar & Haviv)
- Three tests: **appropriate** (right feeling for the audience; not necessarily literal), **distinctive and memorable**
  (unusual enough to hook the mind, sometimes even a bit awkward), **simple** (works at every size, from favicon up).
- The daily tension is between simple and distinctive: simple shapes are common; the job is to find the one simple
  form that is still yours.
- The logo should not try to say everything about the company.
- The "doodle test": can someone draw it from memory?

## Michael Bierut (Pentagram)
- The logo as an empty vessel that fills with meaning over time.
- Logo types: wordmarks, pictorial marks, abstract marks. Choose by how the brand will be used, not by taste.
- Design the system, not just the mark: an identity that flexes (color, form, application) lasts longer than one
  fixed picture.

## What makes logos look AI-generated (practitioner consensus, 2023–2026)
- Pattern averaging: generators recombine the most common features of a category, so results look like everything.
- Over-symmetry and over-balance; designers deliberately break symmetry for tension.
- Default effects: gradients, glow, glossy 3D, swirls and hexagons, "orbit" rings, sparkles.
- Broken or pseudo letterforms; misaligned text on curves (documented in a 2023 Visual Communication study of AI logos).
- "Looks good, means nothing": no idea that can be stated.
- Fails when resized, simplified to one color, or placed in real mockups.
- Legal risk: near-copies of existing marks.

## How designers avoid it
- Start from a brief and a written idea, not from an image.
- Choose a construction method (grid, unit, tool logic, real type) and follow it consistently.
- Work flat and in one color first; color comes last.
- Test in real formats (icon masks, notification bar, WhatsApp preview, shop sticker, receipts) before polishing.
- Redraw by hand/code anything that came from a reference; keep the generator script so decisions are traceable.
- Compare against competitors and stock icon sets; if it could be in a free icon pack, push further.

## Using local visual culture without kitsch
- Borrow logic, not ornament: a calligrapher's nib angle and nuqta proportion, a sign painter's offset shadow,
  a truck artist's confidence with saturated color. Avoid pasting peacocks, chamak-patti or flag motifs onto a mark.
- One cultural reference per identity is usually enough; let the voice carry the rest.
